Market making on Null has a specific meaning, and it is narrower than the phrase suggests
elsewhere. You are not quoting bids and offers, and you are not running a bot. You are holding
**Payer**, the pool that stands behind Receiver so that Receiver can take the return of an
oracle tracked asset. In exchange for taking the other side of that exposure, you collect the
funding Receivers pay and the yield on the collateral inside your own pool.

That trade should be understood before it is admired. When the oracle price rises, Receiver
gains and Payer loses. When the price falls, the direction reverses. Your return therefore
depends on whether the funding and yield you collect over time outweigh the price moves
working against you. Payer pays you to carry risk. It does not remove the risk.

To see any of this in the app, turn on **Pro Mode** in the account menu. Spot then offers
**Payer buy** and **Payer sell** alongside the Receiver ticket. With Pro Mode off, Payer is
hidden entirely, which is the right default for anyone who has not yet decided to take this
side of the market.

## What you actually hold

A Payer position is best pictured as a negative size position in the Receiver pool, with the
market's collateral sitting inside that structure. You are long the collateral and short the
tracked exposure at the same time. The size of the short is not a setting you choose. It falls
out of the balance between the two pools, and it changes as other people enter and leave.

The relationship is expressed through two quantities you will see across these docs. `R` is
the assets in the Receiver pool and `P` is the assets in the Payer pool, both measured in
collateral units. Their ratio sets how much exposure each unit of Payer capital is carrying.

```text
short scale = R / P
Payer short notional = p × R / P
```

Read that second line slowly, because it drives every decision that follows. If Receiver holds
three times the assets Payer does, each dollar of Payer value carries roughly three dollars of
short exposure. As coverage thins and the ratio climbs, the same position becomes a larger bet
without you adding a cent. As Receiver drains and the ratio falls, the bet quietly shrinks.
Position size on Payer is a moving quantity.

## What you earn for carrying it

Payers are compensated from two sources, and it helps to keep them apart. The first is the
yield on your own collateral, which you would earn from that collateral in any case. The second
is funding, paid across from the Receiver pool out of the yield its collateral generates.
Combined, the approximate carry on a Payer position, before fees and hedge costs, looks like this.

```text
carry ≈ y × (1 + R / P)
```

Here `y` is the collateral yield. The opening `y` is the return on your own collateral, and the
`y × R / P` term is the funding arriving from Receivers, scaled by the same ratio that scales
your short. This is why thin coverage cuts both ways. It raises the funding you receive per unit
of capital at exactly the moment it raises your exposure per unit of capital. The worked numbers
behind the formula are in [Funding and yield](/concepts/funding-and-yield).

## Hedging, and why it is your problem

Many Payers do not want outright price exposure, so they hedge outside Null by buying the tracked
asset, or a perpetual contract on it, at another venue. Done well, the hedge offsets the short
embedded in Payer. What remains is the trade they actually wanted: Null funding, plus yield on
Payer collateral, minus whatever the hedge costs to hold.

Null does not open, size, or manage that hedge for you, and its economics are entirely separate
from Null funding. An external venue charges its own funding, its own fees, and its own
maintenance margin, and none of those charges appear in Null's Funding APR. A headline rate that
looks generous inside Null can still lose money once hedge funding turns against you or the basis
between the two venues moves. Judge the position on the net of both legs, never on one leg alone.

Sizing also needs maintenance. Because the hedge should match the short notional `p × R / P`, and
because that notional drifts with pool flows, a hedge that was correct on the day you entered can
be materially wrong a week later. Recheck the ratio after large deposits or withdrawals in either
pool, and resize the hedge when the scale has moved.

## Running a Payer position

<Steps>
  <Step title="Read the pools before you commit">
    Check `R`, `P`, the collateral APR, and the funding APR on the market. Together they tell
    you how large a short you are taking on and what you are currently being paid to hold it.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Buy Payer through Pro Mode">
    Use **Payer buy** on the Spot ticket. Entry and exit fees are charged on notional rather
    than on the cash you put in, so the cost scales with `R / P` and deserves a look first.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Place the hedge outside Null, if you want one">
    Match the short notional at an external venue, and remember that its funding, fees, and
    margin calls are yours to manage. An unhedged Payer position is a deliberate directional
    short, not a neutral one.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Watch the carry against its costs">
    Compare Null funding and collateral yield with the funding and fees on your hedge. When
    the net no longer pays for the exposure, exit with **Payer sell** rather than waiting
    for the rate to rescue you.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
Payer can lose most of its value when the oracle price rises and coverage is thin. Funding
softens that outcome over time, but it does not cap it. Size the position for the price move,
not for the rate.
</Warning>
