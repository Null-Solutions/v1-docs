Fees in Null exist to price an option that would otherwise be free. Oracle prices update in discrete steps, and anyone who can deposit or redeem at a price the market has not yet moved away from is, in effect, trading on information the pools have not absorbed. Entry and exit fees charge for that privilege on both sides of the door, because an entry only fee would leave the mirror image attack, redeeming at a stale price to dodge a move that has already happened, completely unpriced.

That purpose shapes where the money goes. The destination depends on the side that pays. A fee paid on the Receiver side is not protocol revenue: it is paid across to the Payer pool, compensating the holders who were on the other side of the stale price. A fee paid on the Payer side goes to the market's `protocolFees` balance and is later swept to the factory owner. Understanding a Null fee therefore means asking two separate questions: how it is sized, and whose balance it lands in.

## The trade fees stop

Settlement only moves when someone interacts, and the price it settles to comes from a signed report the trader submits, which can be up to 60 seconds old. That leaves a window in which a trader can see that the market has moved before the pools have. Without fees, they could sandwich the move: enter the winning side at the old price, let the new price settle, and leave straight away with the difference, taken from the other pool.

Fees on both sides of the door close that window. The entry fee prices getting in at the old price, and the exit fee prices getting out at it, so leaving just before a known loss is not free either. Both are charged on notional, because the gain from a sandwich scales with the exposure taken, not with the cash posted.

With a 0.25% entry fee and a 0.25% exit fee, a round trip costs about 0.5% of notional. A sandwich only pays when the move it captures is bigger than that.

| Trade | Notional | Move captured | Gain | Fees, both ways | Result |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Receiver posts 10,000 | 10,000 | +0.3% | 30 | about 50 | about −20 |
| Receiver posts 10,000 | 10,000 | +1.0% | 100 | about 50 | about +50 |
| Payer posts 1,000, `R / P` = 5 | 5,000 | −0.3% | 15 | about 25 | about −10 |

The last row shows why the Payer fee is scaled by the pool ratio. A Payer posting 1,000 against a pool five times its size takes on about 5,000 of exposure, and a 0.3% fall earns it about 15. Charged only on the 1,000 posted, the fees would come to about 5, and the sandwich would clear about 10 every time. Charged on the 5,000 of notional, it loses money.

When a Receiver pays, the fee goes to the Payer pool: the side that was exposed to the stale price is the side that is compensated.

## The fees pay for Payer rehedging

A Payer's short is the Receiver pool, so every Receiver deposit or redemption is volume the Payers must rehedge on another venue. The Receiver fees are charged on that same notional and paid to the Payer pool, so they scale one for one with the rehedging they compensate. A 10,000 Receiver trade means Payers rehedge 10,000. With a 0.10% fee and a 0.07% cost to rehedge, it pays Payers 10 for 7 of hedging work. See [Payers](/payers#receiver-fees-pay-for-your-rehedging).

## How the charge is sized

Entry and exit fees are quoted in basis points, but they are not charged on the raw amount handed over. They are charged on notional, worked out with `feeOnTotal`, and the distinction matters most exactly when a trade matters most. The notional base is grossed up to Receiver exposure: it includes the assets being added, so a depositor pays on the full position they are about to hold rather than only on the net amount that survives the fee. For a Payer deposit, the base is further scaled by the current pool ratio, because Payer collateral backs a slice of the whole Receiver book. When Receiver assets are large relative to Payer assets, a modest Payer deposit supports a much larger notional, and the fee follows the notional it actually puts at risk.

Given that base, the arithmetic is the same for deposits and redemptions:

```text
notional = assets * settleableR / ownAssets
fee      = min(assets, ceil(notional * feeBps / (10000 + feeBps)))
```

`settleableR` is the Receiver pool above `MIN_POOL_ASSETS`, and `ownAssets` is the pool the caller is entering or leaving. On a deposit both include the assets being added (a Payer deposit adds only to `ownAssets`). The divisor is `10000 + feeBps`, not `10000`, because the fee is taken out of the amount: at 25 bps a notional of 1,000 pays about 2.49, not 2.50. The result is rounded up and can never exceed the assets of the trade itself. A deposit whose fee would consume the whole amount, so that it would mint no shares, reverts with `ZeroShares`.

A deposit has zero notional, and so pays no entry fee, while the Payer pool is at or below `MIN_POOL_ASSETS`: there is no counterparty to price the option against. Exits are still charged in that state.

Nothing about the fee depends on elapsed time, pool history, or the funding model. It is a toll on the act of changing the pools' composition at a settled price, charged identically whether the market has been quiet for a week or has just moved.

## Where the charge goes

The destination is fixed in the market code and depends only on the side that pays. There is no factory flag or per-market setting for it.

| Fee paid by | Credited to |
| --- | --- |
| Receiver deposit or redemption | The Payer pool |
| Payer deposit or redemption | `protocolFees` |

A Receiver fee is a transfer between the two sides of the same market and never leaves it. A Payer fee is protocol revenue and waits in `protocolFees` to be collected.

There is one exception, and it follows from the settlement floor rather than from fee policy. If the Payer pool is at or below `MIN_POOL_ASSETS`, a Receiver fee is credited to the Receiver pool instead. A Payer pool in that state does not settle, so it takes no price risk and has not earned the fee, and crediting it could leave the fee with no shareholder to own it. The fee is still deducted from the trade: it stays with the Receiver holders as a group, not with the caller alone. Payer fees have no such exception and always go to `protocolFees`.

Accrued protocol fees leave a market through `sweepFees`, which sends the recorded `protocolFees` balance to the factory owner. `sweepFees` does not settle the market and needs no oracle price: it sweeps what the last settlement recorded. Anything else that lands in that balance travels with it, including collateral donated while both pools stood empty. Such a donation is counted into `protocolFees` only by the next [Settlement](/protocol/settlement), which any deposit or redemption triggers, so it can be swept only after that.

## Fees inside settlement and outside it

It helps to keep entry and exit fees separate from the transfers that settlement performs. Settlement moves collateral between pools because prices moved and yield accrued, and it would do so whether or not any individual trade occurred. Fees, by contrast, attach to the deposit or redemption itself, are calculated once that trade's notional is known, and are taken whether the preceding settlement moved a great deal or nothing at all. A trader in a quiet market still pays them, because the option being priced is the ability to act at the settled price before the next price movement arrives.

This is also why fees are described as being on total notional rather than on the amount received. The economic exposure a depositor acquires is the gross position, and pricing the stale price option on anything smaller would systematically undercharge exactly the largest and fastest entrants. Redeemers are charged symmetrically on the exposure they are giving up, which closes the otherwise attractive strategy of waiting for news and leaving at the pre news price for free.

Receivers and Payers do not experience these fees identically. A Receiver fee is scaled by straightforward Receiver notional, while a Payer fee is scaled by the pool ratio, so the same cash deposit can attract very different charges on the two sides when the pools are unbalanced. When Payer collateral is scarce relative to Receiver assets, Payer entry is backing a large book and is priced like it. That is intentional, but it means a quoted basis point figure is not a complete description of cost until the current pool ratio has been applied to it.

## Setting fees

Each market has four fees: an entry fee and an exit fee for Receivers, and an entry fee and an exit fee for Payers. The two sides are independent, so a market can charge Receivers and Payers different rates. A pool charges only the fees of its own side.

Each market starts with the default fees on both sides, and the factory owner can retune a market afterwards by calling `setFees(newReceiverEntryFee, newReceiverExitFee, newPayerEntryFee, newPayerExitFee)` on it. One call sets all four fees. The call settles the market first, so the old fees apply up to that moment. The caps exist so that this discretion cannot become a trap: whatever governance does later, no entry or exit fee on either side can exceed the maximums below, and changing them never rewrites positions already taken.

Entry and exit are the only fees. There is no fee for holding a position: nothing is charged on pool assets over time.

## Matching fees to volatility

A fee is only enough if the round trip costs more than the market usually moves inside the stale window. That depends on the asset, so fees should be set per market and rise with volatility:

| Market | How far it moves in a minute | Fee each way, roughly |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Broad index, gold | Rarely more than a few hundredths of a percent | 0.05 to 0.10% |
| Large-cap stock such as AAPL | Usually under 0.1%, more around news | 0.10 to 0.25% |
| BTC, ETH | Can reach 0.5% or more in a fast market | 0.25 to 0.50% |
| Small caps, volatile tokens | Can reach several percent | 1.00 to 2.50% |

These ranges are illustrative, not settings of any live market. Fees too low for an asset hand value from passive holders to fast traders. Fees too high tax the holders the market is built for. The 2.5% cap bounds the top of the range, so an asset that routinely moves more than about 5% in the stale window is a poor fit for a Null market at any fee.

## Reference

| Constant | Value | Meaning |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| `DEFAULT_ENTRY_FEE` | 25 bps | Entry fee of each side at market creation |
| `DEFAULT_EXIT_FEE` | 25 bps | Exit fee of each side at market creation |
| `MAX_ENTRY_FEE` | 250 bps | Highest entry fee the owner can set, for either side |
| `MAX_EXIT_FEE` | 250 bps | Highest exit fee the owner can set, for either side |
| `MAX_BASIS_POINTS` | 10000 | Basis point denominator |

Those defaults deserve a word of interpretation. At 25 basis points each way, a round trip costs half a percent of notional before funding, vault movement, or any Morpho interest on a leveraged position. That is cheap enough that ordinary rebalancing is not meaningfully discouraged, and expensive enough that depositing and redeeming in rapid succession around every oracle update is a losing habit. The fee schedule is, in effect, a statement about the timescale the market wants its holders to trade on: positions held across settlements, not churn within them.

Because the factory owner can raise fees toward the caps after a market is live, holders also carry a modest governance exposure. The caps bound that exposure tightly, and fee changes cannot touch collateral already settled, but an exit fee raised before you leave is a real cost of leaving. Markets where that trust is misplaced should, over time, simply lose their pools to markets whose owners behave better, which is the discipline a permissionless factory is supposed to provide.
